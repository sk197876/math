## Overview

Numbering and printed pages follow [Federer1969](../bibliography.md#federer1969),
read with [Federer1987Erratum](../bibliography.md#federer1987erratum).
This section develops test functions, distributions, differential forms and
currents. Its formalisation follows the book's definitions and reuses the
smooth calculus, compact cutoffs, partitions of unity, measures and integrals
proved in Chapters 1–3. Objects have independent abstract specifications;
existence proofs precede opaque sealing.

The literal final topology on test functions requires care.
[Menne2016](../bibliography.md#menne2016), Definition 2.13 and Remarks
2.14–2.17, distinguishes it from the locally convex inductive-limit topology.
The latter is coarser. Both have the same continuous linear maps into locally
convex spaces and thus the same continuous real dual. The formalisation
preserves that distinction and makes no assertion that the literal final
topology is a vector topology.

The local continuity criterion uses the same compact set on both sides of
the printed bound. Its constants and derivative orders are nonnegative
integers, as in the book. The proof does not replace this condition by a
bound on a larger compact set. The weakly convergent dual-sequence theorem
uses an actual locally finite smooth sum to rule out supports escaping
every compact subset.

The difference-quotient proof places all sufficiently small translates in
one compact support set. The Taylor remainder tends to zero in each of its
original derivative seminorms. Convergence on that fixed-support step then
gives convergence in Federer's final topology. The integral theorem uses
weak dual measurability and the original locally integrable dual norm;
separability supplies measurable pairings. The Euclidean measure adapters
prove all required Borel, compact-finiteness and translation laws for the
actual Lebesgue measure. Integration by parts reuses the earlier scalar
integral and class-one product rules.

**Correction on p. 344.** The printed nonembedding sentence omits a
zero-dimensional source. If $X=\mathbf R^0$, $U=X$ and $Y=\mathbf R$,
then $U$ is nonempty, $Y$ is nonzero and $U$ is compact. Hence every smooth
function is a compact test, and both spaces have the same topology. The
checked counterexample uses the actual Banach spaces $\mathbf R^0$ and
$\mathbf R^1$. The corrected theorem assumes a nonzero source and a nonzero
target, with nonempty open $U$. More generally, a noncompact open domain and
a nonzero target suffice. See the [error catalogue](../references/federer-errors.tex).

The phrases “usually not a homeomorphic embedding” and “usually not
surjective” on p. 345 remain qualified source observations. Federer states
no universal hypotheses for them. Their precise positive counterparts—the
fixed-support embedding and the compact-support image inclusion—are proved.

The degree-zero and empty-domain regressions are in
[DistributionExamples.v](../../tests/DistributionExamples.v). The concrete
noncompact-support example and zero-dimensional correction retain their
actual sealed source and target spaces.

### 4.1.4 — Distribution constancy (pp. 348–349)

[DistributionConstancy.v](../../theories/DistributionConstancy.v) proves the
original constancy theorem. Let $A$ be a connected open subset of open
$U\subset\mathbf R^n$. If the support of each actual coordinate derivative
$D_jT$ lies in $U\setminus A$, there is a continuous linear functional
$\alpha:Y\to\mathbf R$ such that

$$T(f)=\int_U\alpha(f(x))\,d\mathscr L^n x$$

for every original $f\in\mathscr D(U,Y)$ supported in $A$. The statement
retains Federer's literal integral over $U$ and the original test space,
with the erratum correction on p. 348, line 26. It includes empty $A$,
dimension zero and arbitrary Banach targets. Separability of the whole
space $Y$ or its dual is not assumed.

The proof follows Federer. Fix $a\in A$. The component $C_\varepsilon$ of
$a$ in the original inward locus grows as $\varepsilon$ decreases. The
union of these components is open and relatively closed in connected
$A$, by the earlier connected-ball and component theorems. It is therefore
$A$. Compact finite covers and the directed finite-upper-bound theorem
then place each compact subset in all sufficiently small components.
The same argument applied to the existing compact neighbourhood exhaustion
gives the relatively compact connected subdomains needed for localization.

The inward locus is specified by positive ball margins. For nonempty
complement this is proved equivalent to the original strict set-distance
condition. If the complement is empty, every positive radius is allowed;
no finite distance to the empty set is assigned. Both inward loci and

The summand is specified as a partial function. At a point of $A$ it has a
value exactly where $\mathrm Df(x)$ exists, and this value is
$\operatorname{sign}\det\mathrm Df(x)$; a critical point with an existing
differential keeps the value zero. Off $A$ the summand is zero. The degree
is the exact signed numerical sum of this summand over the whole fibre, as
in 4.1.25. Deleting zero summands does not change such a sum, so the
degree is literally the printed sum over $A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}$; no
differential is required at points outside $A$. Both objects are
independently specified opaque partial functions, and no value is assigned
where the sum is undefined.

The proof follows the book. Since $A$ is bounded, its closure is compact
and $\mathscr L^m(A)<\infty$. Hence
$\xi=\chi_A\,\mathbf e_1\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf e_m$ is an $\mathscr L^m$
summable $m$-vectorfield with compact support, and
$\mathscr L^m\wedge\xi=\mathbf E^m\mathbin{\llcorner}A$. This current is
flat, so its image is the flat Lipschitz pushforward of 4.1.14. By 1.3.4,

$$
[\textstyle\bigwedge_m\mathrm Df(x)]\xi(x)/J_mf(x)
=\chi_A(x)\operatorname{sign}\det\mathrm Df(x)\,\mathbf e_1\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf e_m
$$

wherever $J_mf(x)>0$. The fibre density $\eta$ of 4.1.25 is therefore
$(\operatorname{degree}f|A)(y)\,\mathbf e_1\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf e_m$ at
every $y$ where either side is defined, and the conull domain of $\eta$
is a conull domain of the degree. Since $\mathscr H^m$ and $\mathscr L^m$
agree on $\mathbf R^m$, 4.1.25 gives the current identity. Local
summability of the degree density is derived from 4.1.25, not assumed.
The identity holds for every total function that agrees with the degree
on its domain.

For the constancy assertions, $\partial\mathbf E^m=0$ gives
$\partial(\mathbf E^m\mathbin{\llcorner}A)=-\partial(\mathbf E^m\mathbin{\llcorner}\mathbf R^m\sim A)$.
Since $\operatorname{spt}(\mathbf E^m\mathbin{\llcorner}A)\subset\operatorname{Clos}A$,

$$
\operatorname{spt}\partial(\mathbf E^m\mathbin{\llcorner}A)
\subset\operatorname{Clos}A\cap\operatorname{Clos}(\mathbf R^m\sim A)
=\operatorname{Bdry}A .
$$

The set $\operatorname{Bdry}A$ is compact, so $f(\operatorname{Bdry}A)$ is
compact and closed, and the support of the pushed boundary lies in
$f(\operatorname{Bdry}A)$. The constancy theorem of 4.1.7 gives, on each
component $W$ of $\mathbf R^m\sim f(\operatorname{Bdry}A)$, a real $c$ with
$\operatorname{spt}[f_\#(\mathbf E^m\mathbin{\llcorner}A)-c\,\mathbf E^m]\cap W=\emptyset$.
Restriction to the open set $W$ keeps the Lebesgue density
representation, and a locally summable density is determined almost
everywhere by its current. Hence $\operatorname{degree}f|A=c$ at
$\mathscr L^m$ almost every point of $W$. In ambient dimension zero there
is no boundary; the zero-dimensional constancy theorem supplies the
constant directly.

If $A$ is connected and $f(A)$ misses $f(\operatorname{Bdry}A)$, then the
connected set $f(A)$ lies in a single component, and the preceding
conclusion applies. Empty $A$ and null images are included. If, in
addition, $f$ is univalent on $A$, each fibre $A\cap f^{-1}\{y\}$ has at
most one point. Thus $(\operatorname{degree}f|A)(f(x))=\operatorname{sign}\det\mathrm Df(x)$
whenever $x\in A$ and $\mathrm Df(x)$ exists. The points of
$A\cap\{x:J_mf(x)>0\}$ where the sign differs from $c$ map into an
$\mathscr L^m$ null set, and the area formula 3.2.3(1) makes their set
$\mathscr L^m$ null. Injectivity is required only on $A$.

f:\mathbf R^m\to\mathbf R^n,\qquad
f(x)=u_0+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i}x_j\right)(u_i-u_{i-1}),

$$

$$

of $\mathbf R^n$ contains $\{u_0,\dots,u_m\}$. With
$\zeta=(u_1-u_0)\wedge(u_2-u_1)\wedge\dots\wedge(u_m-u_{m-1})$,

$$
\langle e_1,\mathrm Df(x)\rangle\wedge\dots\wedge\langle e_m,\mathrm Df(x)\rangle
=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}x_i^{m-i}\right)\zeta
\qquad\text{for }x\in\mathbf R^m,
$$

and 4.1.25 gives $[u_0,\dots,u_m]=\mathscr H^m\wedge\eta$, where
$\eta(y)=\zeta/|\zeta|$ for $y\in f(C)$ and $\eta(y)=0$ for

The map is specified by its pointwise formula before its existence is
proved, and is then sealed. Its barycentric coefficients are the successive
differences of the prefix products, and the full product is the last
coefficient. Ratios of positive tail sums recover every positive barycentric
family, which proves the image formula for every family of vertices.
Barycentric coefficients are unique when the edges $u_i-u_0$ are linearly
independent; the cube coordinates are then recovered, and $f|C$ is
univalent.

The printed univalence hypothesis is used literally. The formalisation
states it as the absence of an $(m-1)$ dimensional linear subspace $W$ and
a point $a$ with $u_i-a\in W$ for $i=0,\dots,m$. Whenever $0<m\le n+1$, this
condition is equivalent to linear independence of the edges: a dependent
family spans a subspace of dimension less than $m$, and a basis of that
subspace extends inside the coordinate basis to dimension $m-1$;
conversely, independent edges in an $(m-1)$ dimensional subspace would
exceed its dimension. Under this condition $f|C$ is univalent.

If $n+1<m$, then no $m-1$ dimensional subspace of $\mathbf R^n$ exists, so
the printed condition holds vacuously. Univalence then fails: for $n=0$ and
$m=2$ the map $f$ is constant, and the distinct points
$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and $(\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$ of $C$ have the same image.
This is a degenerate case of the literal wording, not an error of the book;
the intended hypothesis is affine independence of $u_0,\dots,u_m$. For
$m=0$ the condition is false under the natural truncation of $m-1$, and
univalence is trivial. The supplement records this qualification.

The differential columns expand in the consecutive edges by an upper
triangular coefficient family. The earlier exterior expansion of 1.3.4
leaves the product of the diagonal coefficients, which gives the wedge
formula at every point of $\mathbf R^m$, including the coordinate

divided out. The formula applies the published correction on erratum
p. 7, book p. 384 line 19: the last factor is $\langle e_m,\mathrm Df(x)\rangle$,
not the printed $\langle e_m,wf(x)\rangle$.

The current identity follows the preceding cube-product and point-join laws
of 4.1.8 and 4.1.11: the prefix map is the cone over the parametrisation of
the tail, and the cube current is the Cartesian product of the unit
segment current and the cube current of one dimension less. The
identity holds for every family of vertices, including degenerate ones and
the nonzero point current in degree zero.

For the Hausdorff formula, the earlier compact plateau gives a globally
Lipschitzian map with the same values and the same differential near the
closed cube; the polynomial $f$ itself need not be globally Lipschitzian.
The area theorem 4.1.25 then applies to the compact summable
characteristic field of $C$. The exact fibre sum keeps every original
summand. Off the cube the coefficients are zero, and univalence is used

target.

The final density-current identity permits every family of vertices. For

orientation. For $\zeta=0$ the image $f(C)$ is $\mathscr H^m$ null, the
printed quotient is undefined, and the formalisation chooses the zero

### 1.8.1 — Mass, comass, convex hulls, and estimates

The statements on printed pp. 38–39 are formalised for finite-dimensional
real inner-product spaces and positive exterior degree. Mass and comass are
specified by upper-bound and positive-error approximation laws; their real
supremum constructions are hidden by [`MassSealed.v`](../../theories/MassSealed.v) and [`ComassSealed.v`](../../theories/ComassSealed.v).
Downstream proofs use the specifications. Many estimates remain valid in
arbitrary dimension once the specified norms are supplied; finite bases are
required only where the statements and existence arguments need them.

The convex argument is proved locally. A nontrivial linear relation among
too many points permits elimination of a coefficient while preserving the
represented vector and decreasing the total nonnegative weight. This reduces
the number of points to the exterior dimension. Compactness follows from
finite-dimensional compactness of the coefficient simplex and the simple unit
ball. A nearest point supplies the separating covector through its variational
inequality. No external convex separation or Caratheodory theorem is imported.
Scaling a representation of x/mass(x) then gives an optimal decomposition;
the dual bound forces its total length to equal mass(x).

For the sharp comass wedge bound, a simple unit test vector is lifted to the
top exterior power of its associated subspace. Restricting the simple covector,
applying the duality simplicity theorem and Hodge isometry, and pushing forward
again gives a simple contraction of the required norm. The characteristic
pairing identity gives the estimate; graded commutation handles the other
simple factor. All wedge norm statements use positive factor degrees.

**Boundary corrections and conventions.** Literal simplicity in degree zero
means the empty product 1. Thus Federer's unsymmetrised supremum on p. 38
gives comass(c)=c for a scalar zero-form, and comass(-1)=-1. The checked
results [`literal_zeroth_comass`](../../theories/ComassZeroDegree.v#L40) and
[`literal_zeroth_comass_negative`](../../theories/ComassZeroDegree.v#L57)
exhibit this failure of the norm assertion. no alternative scalar convention
is silently substituted. The comparison |phi| <=
sqrt(binomial(n,m))*comass(phi) avoids an inverse zero factor when m exceeds
n.

The representation with positive coefficients of total one and N bounded by
the exterior dimension requires that dimension to be positive. In dimension
zero, N must be zero and the coefficient sum is zero, as proved by
[`zero_dimensional_positive_convex_impossible`](../../theories/MassPositiveConvexRepresentation.v#L76). The hull equality and optimal
mass decomposition still hold in dimension zero: the latter allows an empty
sum. These are local corrections, not entries in Federer's May 1987 erratum.
Regressions cover above-ambient degrees (including zero ambient dimension),
the empty decomposition, orthonormal top vectors, and the negative scalar
counterexample on the zero space.

It does not state the remaining wedge and map estimates in that chapter. The
comments cite its algebra and duality results separately where they support
the proof. The Source context remain those recorded in
`docs/references/README.md`.

### 1.8.2 — Hermitian products and Wirtinger's inequality

The entire item on printed pp. 39–40 is formalised. A Hermitian product is
specified by the book's real bilinearity, i-linearity in its second argument,
conjugate symmetry, and positive diagonal. Its real part is an inner product;
its imaginary part is an alternating two-form. Complex absolute value is
specified by nonnegativity and its square, with existence hidden behind
[`ComplexModulusSealed.v`](../../theories/ComplexModulusSealed.v). The coordinate example is checked against these
abstract laws rather than used to define every Hermitian space.

The proof follows Federer's reduction to the associated real 2m-dimensional
space. The existing alternating two-form normal form supplies orthonormal
pairs and nonnegative coefficients. Hermitian Cauchy bounds each coefficient
by one and identifies its equality case. Pairwise commutation and square-zero
of the two-form blocks give the factorial power formula by a local algebraic
induction. Equality forces the positive paired volume and every coefficient
one. For arbitrary length, scaling a first complex vector by a real square
root absorbs the positive factor into the exact paired representation.

The converse uses an orthonormal paired basis and the positive complex
orientation theorem of 1.6.6. Restricting to the complex span of the factors
removes any finite ambient dimension hypothesis. The inequality and equality
criterion apply in arbitrary ambient dimension. For comass, an independent
complex m-frame expresses the dimension condition and supplies a normalized
paired product attaining the factorial; the public finite-dimensional comass
is then identified by uniqueness of its supremum specification.

Zero vectors are included in positive degree. In degree zero the only literal
simple vector is the empty product, with norm and evaluation one; the result
does not assume the invalid degree-zero norm convention discussed in 1.8.1.
The equality statement retains the signed pairing: reversing the orientation
of a nonzero paired volume does not attain its positive upper bound.
Regressions check this sign, the zero vector, the empty paired product, and
the degree-zero comass on the zero complex coordinate space.

the normal-form and polarity prerequisites retain their earlier precise source
comments. Source context are recorded in `docs/references/README.md`. The
local May 1987 erratum has no correction to this item.

### 1.8.3 — The convex comass unit ball and its extreme-point question

The displayed covector ball on printed p. 40 and its asserted convexity are
formalised by [`comass_unit_ball`](../../theories/ComassUnitBall.v#L26), [`comass_unit_ball_evaluations`](../../theories/ComassUnitBall.v#L31), and
[`comass_unit_ball_convex`](../../theories/ComassUnitBall.v#L42). The proof expresses the ball as the intersection
of the evaluation half-spaces over simple unit vectors. It uses the comass
supremum laws directly and therefore also applies conditionally under the
literal degree-zero convention. [`euclidean_comass_unit_ball`](../../theories/EuclideanComassBall.v#L23) gives the exact
R^n instance through the sealed public comass for positive degree, with
convexity and nonemptiness proved for every n, including zero.

Federer's following sentence, “What are their extreme points?”, is a question,
not a classification theorem. [`is_extreme_point`](../../theories/ComassUnitBall.v#L69) defines its usual open-segment
meaning. No general classification is asserted. The regression
[`zero_space_comass_ball_extreme_point`](../../tests/ExteriorExamples.v#L1471) checks the degenerate singleton case:
in positive degree on R^0 the sole covector is zero and it is extreme.
Thus the definitions and mathematical assertion in this numbered item are
covered; the question is retained as a question.

The Source context are recorded in `docs/references/README.md`; the local
erratum has no correction to this item.

### 1.8.4 — Mass of products supported in orthogonal subspaces

The product equation on printed pp. 40–41 is formalised by
[`orthogonal_mass_product`](../../theories/OrthogonalMass.v#L20), with its public norm instance
[`mass_orthogonal_product`](../../theories/OrthogonalMassInterface.v#L18). The ambient inner-product space is finite-dimensional,
as stipulated at the start of 1.8.1 on p. 38. The factors are actual images
under the exterior inclusion maps of mutually orthogonal specified subspaces.
Either factor may be simple; the other may be arbitrary. Both degrees are
positive, following the explicit norm convention in 1.8.1.

The proof follows the book's displayed pairing argument. The supporting
[`annihilating_block_pairing`](../../theories/AnnihilatingBlockPairing.v#L60) extends the dual-block contraction formula to an
arbitrary second multivector in the annihilated subspace. The first
orthonormal block has a simple polarity covector of comass one. A covector
attaining the second factor's mass and the sharp simple-factor comass wedge
bound yield the lower bound. The mass wedge bound from 1.8.1 gives the upper
bound. Orthonormal factorisation and absolute homogeneity handle arbitrary
simple factors. [`mass_wedge_swap`](../../theories/MassWedgeSymmetry.v#L36) supplies the second-simple-factor case.

[`mass_attained`](../../theories/MassAttainment.v#L107) proves attainment on the closed comass unit ball by local
compactness. Via the polarity isomorphism this ball is an intersection of
closed evaluation half-spaces, bounded by the binomial norm comparison.
Finite-dimensional bounded compactness then gives a maximizer. No external
norm duality or separation theorem is imported.

The proof's auxiliary choices are made explicit:
[`simple_orthogonal_calibration`](../../theories/SimpleOrthogonalCalibration.v#L23) supplies the orthonormal one-covectors
annihilating the other subspace and pairing to the first factor's mass when
that factor is nonzero. [`positive_mass_attained_on_comass_sphere`](../../theories/MassUnitAttainment.v#L19) gives a
unit-comass maximizer when the mass is positive;
[`mass_attained_on_comass_sphere`](../../theories/MassSphereAttainment.v#L55) also includes a zero vector when the degree
does not exceed the ambient dimension.

**Boundary qualification.** The product equation includes zero factors, but
the proof's choices of orthonormal frames and unit covectors cannot always be
made in degenerate cases. Above the ambient dimension every covector is zero,
so no unit-comass covector exists; [`no_unit_comass_above_dimension`](../../tests/ExteriorExamples.v#L1519) checks this,
including the zero ambient space. Closed-ball attainment is still valid and
suffices for the equation. This is a local qualification of the auxiliary
proof, not an entry in Federer's erratum. Regressions also cover a zero wedge
factor, attainment by the zero covector, and the unrestricted-first-factor,
second-simple-factor branch.

Federer's final sentence asks whether multiplicativity holds without either
simplicity hypothesis. That question is retained as a question; the
formalisation does not assert an unrestricted equation.

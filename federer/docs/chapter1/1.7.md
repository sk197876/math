The principal-axis proof follows Federer's successive maxima. The stationary
identity is proved algebraically: the positive semidefinite form `S(x,x) B -
S` on the constraint subspace has zero value at the maximising unit vector, so
its mixed values against that vector vanish. Compactness, extreme-value
existence, and continuity of the coordinate quadratic form are proved using
the project's inner-product development and the general topology already used
for 1.7.1.

The orthogonal decomposition uses minimum-dimensional invariant pieces and
induction on the remaining dimension. This makes the finite refinement argument explicit. The specification includes nonzero summands, minimality by inclusion,
pairwise orthogonality, spanning, and a proved uniqueness theorem for the
components. The empty decomposition is allowed. Orthogonal reducibility itself
has no finite-dimension hypothesis.

The two-form normal form uses the already defined wedge product and the dual
inner product of 1.7.2. A family indexed by `(i,false),(i,true)` denotes the
book's consecutive pair of one-covectors; it is orthonormal as one family,
including between different pairs. Coefficients are nonnegative, as in
Federer. The development proves the one-dimensional zero case, explicitly
orients each two-dimensional block, and verifies the final equality of forms
by evaluation.

The injection factorisation permits an infinite-dimensional target. The
hypothesis that the target contains an independent family of source dimension
expresses the book's dimension inequality. A partial orthonormal family is
completed without choosing a basis for the entire target. The displayed
formulas for `g(e_i)` and for `h(e_i)` when the image is nonzero are proved,
including the full-kernel case. Supporting Federer 1.7.4 rather than the
passage's actual location in 1. the formal statement follows Federer and the
correctly stated theorem.

The projection factorisation follows by taking adjoints, as in Federer.
Accordingly, the defining adjoint and orthogonal-projection specifications of
1.7.4, adjoint existence, uniqueness, reversal, and composition are already
proved as supporting results here. The remaining claims of 1.7.4 are recorded
separately below. Principal-axis witnesses, orthogonal decompositions, skew
normal forms, adjoints, and injection-factorisation witnesses are hidden
behind sealed interfaces. No spectral or polar decomposition is imported from
an external mathematics library.

Regression examples cover the empty principal-axis basis, the negative
orientation of a two-dimensional block, completion across a full kernel,
and the two-form normal form in dimension zero.

common finite-dimensional subspace when coordinates are needed. Scalars may
be any real closed field; finite-dimensional dual products and isomorphisms
have explicit basis hypotheses. The zeroth power agrees with the scalar
inner product, and the first power agrees with the original product.

**Local correction to p. 32.** Federer's unqualified assertion that equality
in the product-of-lengths bound holds exactly for mutually orthogonal factors
omits the zero-factor case. The formal criterion is: at least one factor is
zero, or all factors are mutually orthogonal. The family `(0,u,u)` with `u` a
unit vector is a checked counterexample to the printed criterion. The local
May 1987 erratum contains no correction to this sentence; this is recorded as
a local correction, not Federer's erratum.

**Normalisation clarification on p. 33.** The printed sentence calls the
bases “dual orthogonal bases” when asserting `gamma_m(e_lambda)=omega_lambda`.
For their algebraic dual covectors this requires orthonormality: already in
degree one, `beta(e_i)(e_i)=|e_i|^2`, while the dual basis value is one.
For example, replacing a unit basis vector by twice that vector gives values
four and one. The formal product identity uses the polarity images for any
family, and identifies those images with the dual basis only for an
orthonormal basis. The local erratum has no p. 33 correction; this is an
explicit normalisation clarification, not a correction from that erratum.

The general wedge estimate follows the book's grouping by output index, the
binomial bound on the number of contributing pairs, and finite Cauchy–Schwarz.
The supporting combinatorial and coordinate lemmas are proved within the
project.

Regression examples check the unit empty product in dimension zero, the
signed scalar product in degree zero, vanishing above ambient dimension,
and the explicit one-dimensional counterexample `(0,1,1)`.

## 1.7.12 — Second exterior traces

Both displayed identities on printed p. 36, with their proof on p. 37,
are formalised:

- [`exterior_two_trace`](../../theories/ExteriorTwoTrace.v#L21): `2 trace(exterior_2 f) = trace(f)^2 - trace(f composed with f)`.
- [`exterior_two_adjoint_trace`](../../theories/ExteriorTwoAdjointTrace.v#L21): `trace(exterior_2(f+adjoint(f))) = 2 trace(f)^2 - trace(f composed with f) - trace(adjoint(f) composed with f)`.

The first theorem holds over any field with two nonzero. The second uses
the abstract adjoint equation on a finite-dimensional inner-product space;
it applies to any map satisfying that equation. Dimensions zero and one
are included. Both traces are the existing intrinsic traces of 1.4.5,
with auxiliary exterior bases used only as existence witnesses.

[`trace_two_rank_one`](../../theories/TraceTwoRankOne.v#L21) evaluates the degree-two coefficient on rank-one
tensors. [`trace_gamma_product`](../../theories/TraceGammaProduct.v#L20) extends it by bilinearity through the
Hom-tensor isomorphism. The divided-power identity of 1.4.5 then yields
the first formula. This computes the same degree-two coefficient used by
Federer, without repeating his whole determinant-polynomial expansion.
The second proof follows the book's instruction to apply the first to
the sum with its adjoint, using cyclic and adjoint invariance of trace.
[`adjoint_hom_trace`](../../theories/AdjointSquareTrace.v#L17), [`adjoint_square_trace`](../../theories/AdjointSquareTrace.v#L31), and
[`double_trace_scalar_identity`](../../theories/AdjointSquareTrace.v#L48) record the supporting simplifications.

The local May 1987 erratum has no correction to pp. 36–37.

plane map with its adjoint. The last example checks the negative cross

## 1.7.13 — Hilbert spaces, nearest points, and linear functionals

The item on printed p. 37 is formalised without a finite-dimensional
hypothesis. [`is_hilbert_space`](../../theories/HilbertSpaceUP.v#L17) means completeness for the already verified
inner-product metric, expressed through convergence of proper Cauchy filters.
It is a property of the supplied space, not a new global axiom.

The distance to a nonempty set is specified by nonnegativity, the lower-bound
law, and approximation within every positive error. The existence proof uses
real infima and is sealed. [`inner_set_distance_inf`](../../theories/InnerSetDistance.v#L34) identifies the public
value with the infimum appearing in the book.

For nonempty closed convex [`C`](../../theories/ComplexScalarSealed.v#L19), [`hilbert_nearestP`](../../theories/HilbertNearest.v#L31) and
[`hilbert_nearest_unique`](../../theories/HilbertNearest.v#L44) give the unique nearest point, and
[`hilbert_nearest_distance`](../../theories/HilbertNearest.v#L37) gives the printed equation `d = |a-c|`.
The proof follows Federer's sets `C_s`: they are nonempty, closed, nested,
and have diameter at most `2s` by the midpoint/parallelogram identity.
A direct general-topological Cauchy-filter argument supplies their common
point. No external Hilbert-space or convex-projection theorem is imported.
The witness is hidden behind the nearest-point laws.

[`inner_nearest_subspace_orthogonal`](../../theories/InnerNearestSubspace.v#L27) proves `(a-c).x = 0` for every `x` in
an arbitrary vector subspace when `c` is a nearest point. It evaluates the
printed quadratic at its minimising scalar, using the local completed-square
identity from 1.7.1. Completeness and closedness are needed for existence,
not for this consequence of minimality.

[`hilbert_functional_vectorP`](../../theories/HilbertFunctional.v#L29) and [`hilbert_functional_unique`](../../theories/HilbertFunctional.v#L36) give the unique
vector `u` representing any continuous linear functional by `f(v)=u.v`.
The existence proof follows Federer: for nonzero [`f`](../../theories/ProductSpaceMaps.v#L68), normalise `a` so that
`f(a)=1`, find its nearest point `c` in the closed subspace `ker(f)`, and use
`u = |a-c|^(-2)(a-c)`. The zero functional is handled separately. The
representation equation, construction, sealing, and public interface are
separated.

This source-number discrepancy is recorded separately from Federer's erratum.

Regression examples cover the whole-space and singleton nearest points,
the zero functional, and the representing vector in dimension zero.
